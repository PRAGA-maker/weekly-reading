# Weekly Reading List, 07-29-26

This week leans geometric. Four of the twenty argue, from different directions, that the object you should be studying is not the parameter or the loss but the quotient left after you divide out the symmetry that leaves the function unchanged, and two of them disagree about whether the Fisher metric fixes the problem or is exactly where the problem lives. The target trial section is unusually strong on definitions: one paper separates the "ideal" trial from the "target" trial and shows the gap is a bias source people import on purpose. Recent picks are dated late July 2026 on arXiv; each domain closes with one verified older gem. Network access to arXiv, the arXiv API, and PubMed all worked.

## A. Bayesian statistics

### 1. Constructing and extending n = 1 Bayesian confidence intervals for location parameters in location-scale families
David Gerard. arXiv 2607.25007, 2026-07-27. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25007

**The move:** Explains the folklore that a single draw from a normal with unknown mean and unknown variance still admits a non-trivial confidence interval for the mean, by deriving it two ways as a Bayesian object.

**Why it is worth your time:** The n = 1 interval always looks like a magic trick pulled fully formed from a hat. Gerard builds it as a credible interval under a prior whose coverage becomes exactly valid as the confidence level goes to one, and separately as a t-interval augmented with a prior anchor that equals inverting a Bayes-factor test. The second reading transfers to n greater than or equal to 2 and beats the Student t-interval in expected squared width where prior knowledge is real. It is a clean lesson in how a frequentist coincidence is a Bayesian identity in disguise.

**Cost:** 40 min, moderate, needs comfort with pivots and location-scale families.

**Pairs with:** last week's likelihood-principle entries and any confidence-distribution reading.

### 2. Proper Bayes minimax multiple shrinkage estimation
Pankaj Bhagwat, William E. Strawderman, Edward I. George. arXiv 2607.23717, 2026-07-26. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23717

**The move:** First construction of proper Bayes estimators that are minimax and shrink adaptively toward the best of several targets, using mixtures of square-root superharmonic marginals rather than superharmonic ones.

**Why it is worth your time:** Multiple-shrinkage estimators pick whichever of several prior guesses looks most promising while never doing worse than the MLE, but until now the minimax versions were only formal Bayes, not proper. The square-root superharmonic trick is the load-bearing idea and it yields concrete estimators, including an adaptive convex combination of classical Strawderman estimators. This is Stein-era estimation theory still producing genuinely new existence results, and the construction is teachable.

**Cost:** 60 min, hard, needs superharmonic priors and minimaxity.

**Pairs with:** shrinkage geometry and empirical Bayes; the shell-geometry shrinkage paper from 06-25.

### 3. Inference on counterfactual distributions using martingale posteriors
Gregor Steiner, Mark Steel. arXiv 2607.24143, 2026-07-27. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24143

**The move:** Puts the entire counterfactual outcome distribution, not just the average effect, inside the martingale-posterior framework of Fong, Holmes and Walker, with a Dirichlet-process-mixture predictive.

**Why it is worth your time:** Average effects hide the shape of the effect distribution. By replacing posterior-over-parameters with predictive recursion, the method quantifies epistemic uncertainty on any functional of the counterfactual law, quantiles included, and inherits nonparametric robustness. It extends to instrumental variables with one-sided noncompliance and proves convergence via almost conditionally identically distributed sequences. This is the predictive, de Finetti-flavored view of causal inference doing real work rather than being cited as philosophy.

**Cost:** 50 min, moderate to hard, needs predictive Bayes and DP mixtures.

**Pairs with:** the interventional-score and target-trial entries below; martingale posteriors generally.

### 4. Generalised Robust Bayes for Joint Inference of Model and Contamination
Masahiro Fujisawa, Masaki Adachi, Takuo Matsubara. arXiv 2607.25665, 2026-07-28. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25665

**The move:** Turns generalized Bayes from qualitatively robust into quantitatively robust by jointly inferring the model parameter and the contamination proportion through a Hölder divergence on a scaled density.

**Why it is worth your time:** Standard robust generalized Bayes dampens the influence of outliers but cannot tell you how much contamination there was or which points caused it. Hölder-Bayes gets a bounded posterior influence function, a Bernstein-von Mises result, and observation-level detection scores with no external threshold, and it reinterprets the learning-rate temperature as affine volume scaling of the data space. That last reframe is the reason to read it: the tempering knob everyone tunes by hand gets a geometric meaning.

**Cost:** 45 min, moderate, needs divergences and influence functions.

**Pairs with:** last week's cautionary tales on Bayesian predictive inference; robust divergence estimation.

### 5. Bayesianly Justifiable and Relevant Frequency Calculations for the Applied Statistician (under-cited classic)
Donald B. Rubin. The Annals of Statistics 12(4):1151-1172, 1984. https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176346785

**The move:** Argues that frequency calculations, posterior predictive checks and operating characteristics belong inside the Bayesian toolkit, not outside it, and are the honest way to criticize a model you took seriously enough to fit.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is the paper that legitimized checking a Bayesian model against hypothetical replications, and most of the modern Bayesian-workflow discourse is a footnote to it. It reads as fresh now as then because the field keeps rediscovering that a coherent prior does not exempt you from asking whether the model could have produced the data. Cited constantly, read closely by few. Put it next to the robust-Bayes and predictive-check work above and the throughline is one argument, forty years apart.

**Cost:** 40 min, easy to moderate, no heavy machinery.

**Pairs with:** Bayesian workflow, posterior predictive checks, model criticism.

## B. Learning theory and high-dimensional optimization

### 1. Quotient Dynamics, Effective Curvature, and Implicit Bias in Positive Quadratic Networks
Pengcheng Cheng. arXiv 2607.25624, 2026-07-28. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25624

**The move:** Works out the training dynamics of f(x) = x'UU'x entirely on the quotient by the O(r) symmetry of U, showing factor gradient flow projects exactly onto Riemannian gradient flow on the PSD manifold and, in the underdetermined regime, becomes an entropy mirror flow selecting minimum-trace interpolants.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is a mechanism, not a bound. Because the factor gradient is horizontal, the messy overparameterized flow in U is literally the clean flow in Q = UU', and the implicit bias falls out as a Bregman projection in spectral coordinates. It pins down what isotropic small initialization selects and by how much finite step size perturbs it. If you care about why gradient descent picks the solution it does, this is the model to reason from.

**Cost:** 70 min, hard, needs Riemannian gradient flow and mirror descent.

**Pairs with:** the Fisher-geometric sharpness and dead-directions entries; last week's PAC-Bayes on quotient parameter spaces.

### 2. When Can You Correct Distribution Drift in Temporal Graph Generation? A Sharpening-Drift Tension and an Impossibility for Observation-Based Correction
Tianpeng Li, Xuan Guo, Wenjun Wang, Wang Zhang, Pengfei Jiao. arXiv 2607.24662, 2026-07-27. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24662

**The move:** Proves that the degradation of a temporal-graph generator under drift is irreducible and that no corrector measurable with respect to past observations can remove it, then shows extrapolation is strictly worse than doing nothing because the drift is trendless and mean-reverting.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is a genuine impossibility result with the receipts. The masked flow-matching loss decomposes exactly into entropy plus a divergence that blows up on structures rare in training and common at deployment, and the paper measures a clean power law for the trade-off. The sharp part is the proof that the deployment period being observed does not help: any past-measurable corrector leaves at least the conditional variance, and the two conditions under which extrapolation could win both fail. A rare paper that tells you to stop trying.

**Cost:** 45 min, moderate, needs flow matching and basic stochastic-process facts.

**Pairs with:** distribution shift, conformal drift correction, and the honest-limits theme this week.

### 3. Universal Refinement without Interaction: Order-Optimal 1-Bit Mean Estimation
Yuchen Miao. arXiv 2607.24358, 2026-07-27. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24358

**The move:** Shows interaction is unnecessary for order-optimal 1-bit mean estimation under finite central moments: every 1-bit query is fixed in advance and a later-decoded coarse center only changes how the stored refinement bits are read.

**Why it is worth your time:** The intuition is that adaptive, sequential queries should beat a single non-adaptive batch when you have one bit per sample. This settles the Lau-Scarlett open problem the other way, with matching minimax rates split by moment order, via two decoder-side refinement schemes (periodic dyadic residues and shifted random grids). It is a sharp measurement-complexity result of the kind the spec prizes: something believed to need interaction turns out not to.

**Cost:** 50 min, hard, needs quantization and minimax lower bounds.

**Pairs with:** distributed estimation, communication complexity, information-based complexity.

### 4. Dead Directions: Geometric Singular Learning
Tejas Pradeep Shirodkar. arXiv 2606.05957, 2026-06-04. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05957

**The move:** Bridges singular learning theory and information geometry through the dead direction, a unit vector where the Fisher metric degenerates, and recovers Watanabe's real-log-canonical-threshold data in original coordinates from the decay rate of directional Fisher curvature, no Hironaka resolution needed.

**Why it is worth your time:** Singular learning computes its invariants after blowing up coordinates, which is exactly why practitioners never touch it. This paper reads the triple (lambda, m, nu) off a single checkpoint's forward and backward passes via a K-FAC factorization, and carries the rate to the gauge quotient under gradient flow, noting SGD qualifies but standard Adam does not. It is the most direct handle on degenerate learning geometry I have seen, and it doubles as a critique: the non-degeneracy assumption underlying classical information geometry is precisely what overparameterized models violate.

**Cost:** 90 min, hard, needs singular learning theory and some algebraic geometry vocabulary.

**Pairs with:** the Fisher-geometric sharpness and Fisher-widths entries; loss-landscape degeneracy.

### 5. On-line learning in soft committee machines (under-cited classic)
David Saad, Sara A. Solla. Physical Review E 52:4225-4243, 1995. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.52.4225

**The move:** Derives the exact deterministic dynamics of online gradient learning in a two-layer network in the thermodynamic limit, and shows the long plateaus where student units are symmetric and interchangeable before they specialize.

**Why it is worth your time:** Every current paper on saddle-to-saddle dynamics, feature-learning plateaus, and symmetry breaking during training is walking ground this paper mapped in closed form thirty years ago. The order-parameter equations turn learning into a low-dimensional dynamical system with fixed points, plateaus, and escape, which is exactly the language the 2026 papers reach for. Physicists read it; the ML theory community mostly reinvented it. Read the original.

**Cost:** 60 min, moderate to hard, needs statistical-mechanics-of-learning notation.

**Pairs with:** saddle-to-saddle training, singular learning, and the positive-quadratic-network dynamics above.

## C. Target trial emulation

According to PubMed, the four recent picks and the classic below are indexed with the DOIs given.

### 1. The Ideal Trial: Defining Causal Estimands that Balance Relevance and Feasibility in Target Trial Emulations and Actual Randomized Trials
Margarita Moreno-Betancur, Rushani Wijesuriya, John B. Carlin. Epidemiology 37(2):153-162, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001933

**The move:** Separates the ideal trial, the hypothetical perfect experiment that defines the estimand of practical interest, from the target trial, and shows that the popular practice of specifying the target trial to align with the available data quietly changes the estimand.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is the register of "Where do target trials come from," and it sharpens the same nerve. The field's default advice, make the target trial match the data's eligibility and timing, is presented here as a bias source: it mirrors the often-overlooked biases of actual trials relative to the ideal one. The contribution is conceptual and load-bearing. It gives you a vocabulary to say exactly which of your emulation's compromises cost you relevance rather than only feasibility.

**Cost:** 40 min, moderate, needs the estimand-first causal framework.

**Pairs with:** the Cain classic below and the nonmutually-exclusive-assignment paper; estimand specification generally.

### 2. Target trial emulation under nonmutually exclusive assignment: structural pitfalls and methodological remedies
Atsushi Takayama, Shiro Tanaka, Koji Kawakami. American Journal of Epidemiology 195(4):1045-1055, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwag014

**The move:** Studies the case, common and under-examined, where treatment groups are not mutually exclusive because patients can take combination or single-agent therapy or neither, and shows by simulation when the standard emulation recovers the marginal effect and when it cannot.

**Why it is worth your time:** Most causal design assumes clean exposed-versus-unexposed contrasts. Here the group boundaries are genuinely ambiguous, and the paper maps the failure region: with good covariate overlap, nonmutual-exclusivity can match or beat the mutually exclusive design, but with poor overlap even advanced strategies miss the truth. It is a concrete lesson in aligning estimand, design and assignment structure rather than assuming the textbook contrast exists.

**Cost:** 35 min, moderate, needs propensity scores and positivity.

**Pairs with:** positivity diagnostics; the ideal-trial framing above.

### 3. Target Trial Emulation for Evaluating Health Policy
Nicholas J. Seewald, Emma E. McGinty, Elizabeth A. Stuart. Annals of Internal Medicine 177(11):1530-1538, 2024. https://doi.org/10.7326/M23-2440

**The move:** Ports the seven-component target trial protocol to policy evaluation, where the "treatment" is a policy that was never randomized and time zero, eligibility and assignment need rethinking at the unit-of-a-jurisdiction scale.

**Why it is worth your time:** The interesting friction is that policies are assigned to places and times, not patients, so the emulation has to define eligibility and time zero for units that are not people. Laying the policy problem on the trial protocol exposes exactly which threats to causal inference the design does and does not handle. If your work touches longevity or health-system interventions rather than drug exposures, this is the translation guide.

**Cost:** 30 min, easy to moderate, no heavy machinery.

**Pairs with:** difference-in-differences and synthetic control; the misalignment review below.

### 4. Review of methods to deal with the misalignment of times of eligibility, start of follow-up, and treatment assignment in studies explicitly aimed at emulating target trials
Matthew McIntyre, Sally Yaacoub, Elodie Perrodeau, Raphael Porcher, Viet-Thi Tran. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 185:111898, 2025. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111898

**The move:** Audits 199 published emulations for whether the three times that must coincide actually do, and finds about half were misaligned, two-thirds of those uncorrected, and most of the uncorrected cases fixable with a low-cost change.

**Why it is worth your time:** This turns immortal-time bias from a warning into a measurement. The value is the empirical base rate: knowing that misalignment is a coin flip in the published literature, and that cloning-censoring-weighting is the dominant fix when authors bother, calibrates how much to trust an emulation you did not run. It also names the cheap fixes, so it doubles as a checklist.

**Cost:** 30 min, easy, survey-style.

**Pairs with:** the Cain classic on cloning and dynamic regimes; immortal-time-bias reading.

### 5. When to start treatment? A systematic approach to the comparison of dynamic regimes using observational data (under-cited classic)
Lauren E. Cain, James M. Robins, Emilie Lanoy, Roger Logan, Dominique Costagliola, Miguel A. Hernán. The International Journal of Biostatistics 6(2):Article 18, 2010. https://doi.org/10.2202/1557-4679.1212

**The move:** Shows how to emulate a randomized trial of dynamic regimes of the form "start treatment within m months of a time-varying marker first crossing threshold x" by cloning each patient into every regime they are consistent with and censoring at deviation.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is where the clone-censor-weight machinery for grace periods and dynamic "when to start" questions was actually assembled, on the FHDH HIV cohort, years before target trial emulation became a slogan. Everyone cites the later framework papers; this is the one that did the construction and named the complications. Reading it makes the misalignment review above concrete: you see why the three times drift apart and what cloning buys you.

**Cost:** 60 min, moderate to hard, needs g-methods and inverse-probability weighting.

**Pairs with:** the misalignment review and the ideal-trial paper; sustained-treatment-strategy estimands.

## D. Information geometry

### 1. Fisher Widths: Local Learning Geometry and Anisotropic Recovery
Vu Khac Ky. arXiv 2607.20578, 2026-07-22. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20578

**The move:** Defines two Gaussian-width functionals on a statistical manifold, one under the Fisher metric and one under its inverse, and proves they play complementary roles: the Fisher width sizes local estimation fluctuations, the inverse-Fisher width controls anisotropic measurement and sparse recovery.

**Why it is worth your time:** Gaussian width is the standard currency of high-dimensional recovery, and this paper shows the Fisher metric splits it into a learning side and a recovery side that trade off but cannot both shrink below the Euclidean scale, formalized by w_G(T) w_{G^{-1}}(T) greater than or equal to w(T)^2. The consequence that sparse recovery depends on where the active coordinates sit in the Fisher spectrum, not just how many there are, is the kind of geometry-does-real-work result the spec asks for. Remove the Fisher structure and the ordering of supports disappears.

**Cost:** 60 min, hard, needs Gaussian width and statistical dimension.

**Pairs with:** compressed sensing, the Fisher-geometric sharpness entry, and Fisher-information-based experimental design.

### 2. Weak Information Geometry: Riemannian Structures from Distributional Inference Functions and Stein Discrepancies
Rodrigo Labouriau. arXiv 2607.11246, 2026-07-13. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11246

**The move:** Enlarges the class of models that carry a Riemannian structure far beyond the classical Fisher-Rao setting by representing laws as tempered distributions and inducing metrics from Stein-type inference functions.

**Why it is worth your time:** Fisher-Rao needs densities and regularity that many models lack; by working in the space of tempered distributions with Schwartz-kernel instruments, this constructs metrics where Fisher-Rao is undefined. Stein discrepancies, which the sampling community already uses computationally, become the source of geometry. It is a foundations paper that widens the map, and it connects the geometric and the score-based views of inference at the level of definitions rather than analogy.

**Cost:** 75 min, hard, needs distributions in the Schwartz sense and Stein operators.

**Pairs with:** Stein discrepancy methods; the interventional-score entry below.

### 3. Interventional Score Geometry for Causal Inference
Mojtaba Eslami. arXiv 2607.21914, 2026-07-24. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21914

**The move:** Shows the observational score field cannot identify causal direction and builds the interventional analogue, where a hard intervention restricts the law to a submanifold and the score lives on the remaining free coordinates, giving a Fisher metric on families of interventions with a shared target.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is the geometric statement of why observation is not intervention. Two structural models with identical observational scores can have different interventional score derivatives, and the paper gives an explicit bivariate Gaussian pair that does. It yields a geometric dictionary for randomized trials, instrumental variables and conditional-independence designs, placing each on Pearl's ladder. It adds no identification beyond the assumptions, and says so plainly, which is the honesty the spec rewards.

**Cost:** 55 min, hard, needs score matching and structural causal models.

**Pairs with:** the target trial section and the martingale-counterfactual entry; this is the geometry under those designs.

### 4. Fisher-Geometric Sharpness and the Implicit Bias of SGD toward Flat Minima
Md Sakir Ahmed, Kumaresh Sarmah, Hemen Dutta. arXiv 2606.20469, 2026-06-18. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20469

**The move:** Defines flatness through the Fisher metric so that sharpness is invariant under function-preserving reparametrization, answering the Dinh et al. objection that Euclidean flatness can be rescaled away, then shows mini-batch SGD noise concentrates mass at Riemannian-flat minima.

**Why it is worth your time:** The flat-minima-generalize story has always had a hole: Euclidean flatness is not reparametrization invariant, so the claim was ill-posed. Grounding it in the Fisher information matrix repairs the definition, and the paper closes the loop with a stationary-distribution argument and a PAC-Bayes bound controlled by Riemannian sharpness. Read it directly against Dead Directions, which argues the Fisher metric degenerates precisely on the singular set overparameterized nets occupy. One paper leans on the Fisher metric as the fix, the other on where it breaks.

**Cost:** 55 min, moderate to hard, needs Fisher information and PAC-Bayes.

**Pairs with:** Dead Directions and the positive-quadratic-network dynamics; SGD as an SDE.

### 5. I-Divergence Geometry of Probability Distributions and Minimization Problems (under-cited classic)
Imre Csiszár. The Annals of Probability 3(1):146-158, 1975. https://doi.org/10.1214/aop/1176996454

**The move:** Treats KL divergence as a squared distance and proves the projection of a distribution onto a convex set exists and satisfies a Pythagorean identity, all without Lagrange multipliers, plus a general iterative minimization algorithm.

**Why it is worth your time:** This is the information-projection backbone that the dually-flat, e-projection and m-projection language of Amari later sat on top of, and it is cited by the geometry crowd far more than it is read. The Pythagorean theorem for I-divergence is the reason maximum-entropy and minimum-discrimination problems behave like orthogonal projections, and the iterative scheme prefigures alternating-minimization and EM. If you want the geometry that makes exponential-family inference feel like Euclidean projection, start here rather than with a survey.

**Cost:** 50 min, moderate to hard, needs measure-theoretic probability and convexity.

**Pairs with:** the misspecification-projection theme; maximum entropy, EM, and dually flat structure.

## Threads

### Thread 1: Coordinates lie, so study the quotient
Papers: Positive Quadratic Networks (B1), Fisher-Geometric Sharpness (D4), Dead Directions (B4), Fisher Widths (D1), and at a distance Saad-Solla (B5).
Four papers independently insist that the parameter you optimize is the wrong object and the right one is what remains after dividing out symmetry or reweighting by the Fisher metric. Positive quadratic networks make this literal by projecting onto the O(r) quotient; Fisher-geometric sharpness fixes the ill-posed flatness definition by going Riemannian; Fisher widths split complexity into two metric-dependent halves. The productive tension is between D4 and B4: Fisher-Geometric Sharpness treats the Fisher metric as the cure for reparametrization pathology, while Dead Directions shows the Fisher metric degenerates exactly on the singular set overparameterized networks live on. Both cannot be the last word.

### Thread 2: The intervention identifies, not the model
Papers: Interventional Score Geometry (D3), Martingale-posterior counterfactual distributions (A3), The Ideal Trial (C1), Nonmutually exclusive assignment (C2), Cain et al (C5).
Across a geometry paper, a Bayesian predictive paper and three epidemiology papers runs one claim: identification comes from what the intervention is and how the design encodes it, never from the flexibility of the observational model. Interventional Score Geometry proves two models with the same observational score respond differently to do-operations. The Ideal Trial shows aligning the estimand to the data changes what you identify. Cain shows the identifying content is in the cloning-and-censoring construction. The martingale-posterior paper is the constructive counterpart: given the design, estimate the whole counterfactual law, not just its mean.

### Thread 3: Sharp yes and sharp no
Papers: 1-Bit Mean Estimation (B3), n = 1 Bayesian confidence intervals (A1), Temporal-graph impossibility (B2), Proper Bayes minimax (A2), Csiszár projection (D5).
This week is rich in exact boundaries. Two are surprising possibilities: one bit per sample needs no interaction, and one observation still yields a valid interval. One is a hard impossibility: no past-measurable corrector fixes temporal-graph drift. Two are existence results with clean geometry: proper Bayes minimax multiple shrinkage, and the existence and uniqueness of I-projections. The taste this builds is refusing "it probably works" in favor of proving where the wall is, in either direction.

## Synthesis

The center of gravity this week is a single methodological conviction showing up in four fields at once: the coordinates you were handed are not the invariant object. In learning theory it is the O(r) or gauge quotient, in geometry it is the Fisher metric and its degeneracies, in causal inference it is the estimand defined by an ideal intervention rather than by the data you happen to hold. The papers agree that Euclidean or naive parametrizations mislead, and they disagree productively about the fix. Fisher-Geometric Sharpness and Dead Directions stage that disagreement most sharply, one building on the Fisher metric and the other locating its collapse, and reading them together is more instructive than reading either alone.

The causal papers converge from the applied side on the same lesson the score-geometry paper reaches from theory: designs and interventions carry the identifying content, and dressing up an observational model does not add any. The Ideal Trial deserves the extra minute, because it reframes the field's standard advice as a bias you adopt on purpose, which is exactly the kind of reframe this list exists to surface. The week's honesty runs through the impossibility and boundary results, from temporal-graph drift to the one-bit and one-observation surprises, which collectively argue for proving where the wall is rather than reporting that something usually works.

Two questions worth sitting with. First, if the Fisher metric is both the natural cure for reparametrization pathology and the thing that degenerates on the singular sets real networks occupy, what is the right invariant geometry for overparameterized learning, and is it a quotient, a resolved coordinate system, or something that has to track both. Second, the interventional-score and target-trial papers both say identification is a property of the design; the older dynamical-systems view of learning in Saad-Solla says the trajectory carries information a static snapshot does not. Whether estimation geometry and training dynamics are two views of one manifold, or genuinely different objects, is the thread I would pull next.
