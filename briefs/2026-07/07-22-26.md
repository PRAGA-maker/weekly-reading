# Weekly Reading List — 07-22-26

An estimand-and-geometry week. One cluster keeps asking a single question, what exactly are you estimating, and finds that a routinely reported number (a subgroup count, a disparity effect, a nutritional coefficient, even a hazard ratio) is not a well-defined population target until you name the question first. A second cluster is pure Fisher-Rao: the same metric shows up building a prior, separating quantum from classical inference, and relating two independence tests, with Rao's 1945 origin paper underneath all of it. The learning-theory section is about sharp converses, the exact point where a weaker oracle stops being enough. Network confirmed via the arXiv API and listings; PubMed reachable. Every finalist verified against its abstract page or Crossref record. Each domain is four recent papers plus one verified classic.

## A. Bayesian statistics

### 1. Some cautionary tales about Bayesian predictive inference
Emanuela Dreassi, Fabrizio Leisen, Luca Pratelli, Pietro Rigo. arXiv:2607.19206, 2026-07-21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19206

- The move: two errors in the predictive-first program dissected, conflating an exchangeable representation with a real data-generating mechanism, and leaning on asymptotic exchangeability as if it bought more than it does.
- Why it is worth your time: this comes from people who build predictive constructions for a living, so it is an inside critique of the martingale-posterior and predictive-resampling revival, not a drive-by. The value is in the counterexamples that show where "asymptotically exchangeable, therefore fine" quietly fails. It trains the muscle of asking what a de Finetti representation does and does not license you to assume.
- Cost: 35 min, moderate, needs exchangeability and predictive inference.
- Pairs with: finalist A5, the infinite-dimensional version of the same warning.

### 2. Informative Distance-Based Priors for Correlation Matrices Centred on a Target Reference
Anna Freni-Sterrantino, Janet van Niekerk, Elias Teixeira Krainski, Denis Rustand, Haziq Jamil, Håvard Rue. arXiv:2607.18926, 2026-07-21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18926

- The move: put a prior on correlation matrices whose mass decays exponentially in Fisher arc-length from any user-chosen reference matrix, so you can shrink toward a structured target instead of being stuck shrinking to the identity.
- Why it is worth your time: the space of correlation matrices is a curved manifold, and most priors implicitly pick the identity as the simple state without saying so. Using the actual Fisher-Rao geodesic to a reference makes "believe this correlation structure" a first-class prior statement, and casting it as a Penalised Complexity prior keeps the shrinkage honest. It is prior geometry that does real work rather than decoration, and it belongs next to the information-geometry section.
- Cost: 45 min, moderate, needs PC priors and the Fisher metric on the correlation manifold.
- Pairs with: finalist D5, the origin of the metric this prior is built from.

### 3. Amortized Inference for Sampling Distributions Where the Bootstrap Fails
Akash Deep. arXiv:2607.16666, 2026-07-18. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16666

- The move: for the cases where Efron's bootstrap is provably inconsistent (sample maxima on bounded support, means under infinite variance, extreme quantiles, tail-index estimators), train a network on datasets drawn from a prior over a family, scoring single draws of the root by pinball loss so the population minimizer is the target sampling quantile.
- Why it is worth your time: it takes the known catalog of bootstrap-failure cases and offers an amortized replacement that sidesteps the m-out-of-n and subsampling rate corrections, which depend on unknown parameters and misbehave at realistic sample sizes. The taste it builds is recognizing that a universal default silently breaks on exactly the heavy-tailed and extreme problems where you most need it, and that a proper scoring rule can be reverse-engineered to hand you the object you wanted.
- Cost: 50 min, moderate, needs bootstrap consistency theory and proper scoring rules.
- Pairs with: finalist A1, another paper about a trusted procedure failing where nobody looks.

### 4. The Resolution of Causal Heterogeneity
Yuki Ohnishi, Fan Li. arXiv:2607.17280, 2026-07-19. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17280

- The move: the number of subgroups a heterogeneity analysis reports is not an estimand outside genuine latent-class populations, so replace it with the resolution profile, the fewest groups explaining a prescribed fraction of causal heterogeneity, a functional of the causal feature law defined for every population.
- Why it is worth your time: a clean reframe that kills a routine abuse, treating a model-dependent subgroup count as if it were a population quantity, and hands back a well-defined target with cross-fitted Bayesian-bootstrap, influence-function-corrected inference. It is the Bayesian-causal echo of the target-trial section: name the estimand before you estimate, or the number you report answers no question.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs influence functions, cross-fitting, and causal estimand theory.
- Pairs with: finalist C1, the target-trial version of specifying the question first.

### 5. On the Consistency of Bayes Estimates (older classic)
Persi Diaconis, David Freedman. Annals of Statistics 14(1):1-26, 1986. https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176349830

- The move: natural nonparametric location priors, including Dirichlet priors used too aggressively, produce posteriors that concentrate on the wrong answer, so coherence and conjugacy do not buy consistency once the parameter space is infinite-dimensional.
- Why it is worth your time: the canonical negative result that the current nonparametric-Bayes and predictive crowd cites as a talisman and rarely reads. The construction is natural rather than pathological, which is the whole point, and it is the direct ancestor of this week's cautionary-tales paper. It builds the reflex that the guarantees you take for granted in finite dimensions can evaporate, and that Bayesians have their own reasons to care about frequentist consistency.
- Cost: 2 hours, hard, needs measure-theoretic probability and some functional analysis.
- Pairs with: finalist A1, the same warning brought back to the predictive setting eighty years of practice later.

## B. Learning theory and high-dimensional optimization

### 1. PAC-Bayes Bounds on Quotient Parameter Spaces: Geometry-induced Implicit-Bias Priors
Nicola Aladrah, Fabio Anselmi. arXiv:2607.18422, 2026-07-20. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18422

- The move: run PAC-Bayes on the quotient predictor space rather than parameter space, so the KL term stops charging you for differences that are pure reparameterization symmetry, and the leftover geometric volume of each predictor's fiber induces a canonical implicit-bias prior.
- Why it is worth your time: the reframe is that a continuous symmetry is not a nuisance to mod out numerically but the object that determines the correct prior. Pushing prior and posterior to the quotient preserves the Gibbs risks while deleting a nonnegative KL contribution that was there only because of how you happened to coordinatize the same function, and then the fiber volume tells you which reference prior is principled. It touches real overparameterized networks, where these symmetries are everywhere and usually ignored.
- Cost: 75 min, moderate-hard, needs PAC-Bayes and quotient or orbit geometry.
- Pairs with: finalist B5, which of the symmetric critical points the dynamics actually selects.

### 2. The Price of Hidden Curvature: An Ω̃(d^{5/4}√T) Lower Bound for Bandit Convex Optimization
Nived Rajaraman. arXiv:2607.18652, 2026-07-21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18652

- The move: the first regret lower bound for stochastic bandit convex optimization that grows faster than d√T, built from an explicit hard class shaped as a soft maximum of a tube and a squared distance, proving bandit convex optimization is strictly harder than linear bandits.
- Why it is worth your time: a surprising sharp separation with a mechanism. The hard functions hide curvature that no zeroth-order method can cheaply probe, so the extra d^{1/4} is the information cost of curvature rather than a proof artifact. It is the kind of lower bound that reshapes intuition about where the difficulty in derivative-free optimization actually lives.
- Cost: 90 min, hard, needs minimax lower-bound machinery and online convex optimization.
- Pairs with: finalist B5, the same curvature seen from the convergence side, and D1, curvature as the quantity worth isolating.

### 3. Mixing-Free and Signal-Optimal Learning of Gaussian Graphical Models from Glauber Dynamics
Vignesh Tirukkonda, Gautam Dasarathy. arXiv:2607.18559, 2026-07-20. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18559

- The move: recover a Gaussian graphical model's edges from a single trajectory of random-scan Glauber dynamics with a mixing-free estimator that attains the information-theoretic κ^{-2} dependence on edge strength, via a dueling-neighborhood search.
- Why it is worth your time: a believed-hard-is-easy result. Learning from one dependent trajectory is usually assumed to cost the chain's mixing time, which can be super-polynomial in dimension without strong assumptions, and here it does not, while still matching the optimal signal-strength rate. It sharpens the instinct to separate the statistical price of dependence from the computational price of mixing, which are routinely conflated.
- Cost: 90 min, hard, needs graphical-model selection and Markov-chain sample-complexity arguments.
- Pairs with: finalist B4, the other paper this week pinning down the exact price of a weaker source of information.

### 4. The Tractability Landscape of Sampling with Inexact Scores
Anming Gu, Kevin Tian, Hubert Yang, Yusong Zhu. arXiv:2607.19004, 2026-07-21. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19004

- The move: a tight, algorithm-agnostic characterization of which inexact score-oracle error models still permit sampling with vanishing total-variation bias, with the result that anything weaker than the sub-Gaussian error assumption rules unbiased sampling out.
- Why it is worth your time: prior work gave a sufficient condition, this gives the matching converse, which is what tells you a theorem sits at the right level of generality. It says the sub-Gaussian score-error assumption behind diffusion-sampling guarantees is essentially necessary, not merely convenient, and it holds regardless of the algorithm. Directly relevant to when score-based generative models can be trusted to be unbiased.
- Cost: 45 min, hard, needs score-based sampling theory and total-variation arguments.
- Pairs with: finalist B3, both draw a sharp line at the exact strength of oracle that suffices.

### 5. Gradient Descent Converges to Minimizers (older sleeper)
Jason D. Lee, Max Simchowitz, Michael I. Jordan, Benjamin Recht. arXiv:1602.04915, 2016. https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04915

- The move: with random initialization, gradient descent almost surely avoids strict saddle points and converges to a local minimizer, proved in a few pages by importing the Stable Manifold Theorem from dynamical systems.
- Why it is worth your time: the clean cross-domain import behind a fact everyone now invokes loosely, that gradient descent escapes saddles. Remove the stable-manifold argument and the almost-sure statement collapses, which is exactly the test for whether borrowed structure is load-bearing. It is short, teachable, under-read relative to how often the conclusion is cited, and it is the upper-bound counterpart to this week's curvature lower bound.
- Cost: 30 min, moderate, needs basic dynamical systems and smooth optimization.
- Pairs with: finalist B2, the curvature story from below, and B1, which of the minimizers the geometry picks.

## C. Target trial emulation

### 1. Nesting a Target Study within a Target Trial: A Framework for Evaluating Intervention Effects on Disparities
Epidemiology, 2026-07-21. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000002024

- The move: a two-layer construction, a target study that grounds the disparity measure in explicit ethical assumptions and anchors it to a population in calendar time, nested inside a target trial that specifies the intervention.
- Why it is worth your time: it extends the target-trial discipline to disparities, where the estimand problem is worse than usual because "effect on a disparity" has no meaning until you fix a population and an allowability concept for what counts as a fair comparison. The paper forces the causal question to be well posed before any estimation, which is the exact register this section rewards. Read it as a template for stating a hard estimand, not for its application.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs potential outcomes and some familiarity with disparity measures.
- Pairs with: finalist A4, the same insistence on defining a heterogeneity or disparity estimand before estimating it.

### 2. Long-term Cardiovascular Outcomes Following Bariatric Surgery: Reconciling Seemingly Conflicting Evidence
Sebastien Haneuse, Luke Benz, Valerie A. Smith, David Arterburn, Matthew L. Maciejewski. Epidemiology 37(4):467-475, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001952

- The move: take two recent claims-and-EHR studies that found no cardiovascular benefit of bariatric surgery, re-emulate their design on Kaiser Permanente data, recover the protective effect, and then diagnose why the analyses diverged.
- Why it is worth your time: a methodological autopsy rather than another point estimate. It shows that "we emulated a target trial and found null" can itself be an artifact, driven by small-sample statistical validity, transportability limits, and the conservatism of clinical-trial thinking applied where it does not fit. The honest conclusion, that concordance should not always be expected and triangulation beats single-study deference, is the opposite of the field's usual overclaiming.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs selection bias and transportability basics.
- Pairs with: finalist C4, another case where a design choice changes the answer, and the concordance-audit entries in the log.

### 3. What Are We Estimating? Revisiting Standard Nutritional Models Through the Target Trial Framework
Yu-Han Chiu, Lan Wen. American Journal of Epidemiology, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwag053

- The move: show that the standard nutritional-epidemiology regressions, the energy-partition, nutrient-density, residual, and all-components models, are re-parameterizations of the same conditional outcome model that the g-formula uses, and warn that dropping them into the g-formula without updating total energy intake under each intervention produces wrong counterfactuals.
- Why it is worth your time: a reframe that collapses a confusing zoo of named models into one causal object and then names the exact bug. It separates model choice from estimand, and it shows that the coefficient nutritional epidemiologists interpret causally often answers no well-posed question at all. The chicken-versus-fish worked example makes the abstract point concrete.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs the g-formula and time-fixed causal estimands.
- Pairs with: finalist A4 on estimand versus summary, and finalist C1.

### 4. Antibiotics and Preterm Delivery: The Prevalent New-user Cohort Design to Resolve Immortal Time Bias
Simon Galmiche, Eros Comin, Sophie Dell'Aniello, Jacques Balayla, Samy Suissa. Epidemiology 37(3):355-362, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001947

- The move: apply the prevalent new-user design with time-conditional propensity scores, matching each third-trimester antibiotic initiator to a nonuser at the same gestational day, and watch the hazard ratio move from 0.78 under the time-fixed, immortal-time-biased analysis to 1.14 once corrected.
- Why it is worth your time: a legible applied case where the design choice flips the sign of the effect, from antibiotics looking protective against preterm delivery to modestly harmful. The prevalent new-user design is Suissa's answer to immortal time when a clean new-user cohort is infeasible, and this is a worked example you can lecture from. It is the empirical counterpart to the classic that follows.
- Cost: 35 min, low-moderate, needs immortal time bias and propensity matching.
- Pairs with: finalist C5, which explains why the biased hazard ratio was untrustworthy to begin with.

### 5. The Hazards of Hazard Ratios (older classic)
Miguel A. Hernán. Epidemiology 21(1):13-15, 2010. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181c1ea43

- The move: even in a flawless randomized trial, the hazard ratio at each instant conditions on survival to that instant, so it inherits a built-in selection bias and has no clean causal interpretation as a single summary.
- Why it is worth your time: three pages that changed how careful people read survival analysis, and the direct ancestor of every per-protocol and aligned-time-zero argument in the target-trial literature. The reason emulations obsess over time zero is exactly this selection effect. It is under-read relative to how load-bearing it is, and you can absorb the whole argument in one sitting.
- Cost: 15 min, easy, needs only basic survival analysis.
- Pairs with: finalist C4, whose biased hazard ratio is the same object immortal time inflates.

## D. Information geometry

### 1. The Quantum Correction to Gaussian Information Geometry is the Killing Form of the Symplectic Algebra
Christian Kerskens. arXiv:2607.16376, 2026-07-17. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16376

- The move: on the cone of Gaussian covariance matrices, the exact difference between the dual quantum Bures metric and the dual Fisher-Rao metric is independent of the covariance and equals the trace form, proportional to the Killing form, of the symplectic Lie algebra sp(2n,R).
- Why it is worth your time: the cleanest secretly-Y reframe of the week. The quantum correction people recompute case by case is a single covariance-independent object, and it is a Lie-algebraic invariant, so strip the symplectic structure and the theorem is gone. That is the cross-domain-import test passed cleanly, and it says the gap between classical and quantum Gaussian inference is not analytic drudgery but one piece of algebra.
- Cost: 90 min, hard, needs symplectic algebra and the Bures and Fisher-Rao metrics.
- Pairs with: finalist D2, the algebraic foundations of the same metrics, and finalist A2, Fisher-Rao on covariance-type manifolds put to work.

### 2. Metric tensors and two-forms in information geometry from the GNS construction
Marco Castrillón López, Florio M. Ciaglia, Laura González-Bravo, Alberto Ibort. arXiv:2607.15800, 2026-07-17. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15800

- The move: build the information-geometric metric and its associated two-form directly from the GNS representation of a C*-algebra, organizing the GNS Hilbert spaces into non-trivial fibrations over the state space, without assuming that state space is a smooth manifold.
- Why it is worth your time: it pushes information geometry into the noncommutative and quantum setting where the usual move, pulling tensors back from an ambient manifold, simply fails because the state space is not smooth. Recovering both the symmetric metric and the antisymmetric two-form from one construction is the structural reason the quantum and classical metrics differ, which is the fact the Killing-form paper makes concrete. Foundational and demanding.
- Cost: 2.5 hours, hard, needs C*-algebras, the GNS construction, and differential geometry.
- Pairs with: finalist D1, the concrete payoff of this abstract machinery.

### 3. Mutual Information second order expansion is the Pearson's chi-square statistic
Marius Marinescu. arXiv:2607.18425, 2026-07-20. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18425

- The move: a stochastic Taylor expansion of mutual information, via the delta method, whose second-order term is exactly Pearson's chi-square up to a scale factor, which then quantifies the precise gap between the G² likelihood-ratio and χ² independence statistics.
- Why it is worth your time: a small, teachable reframe of the Fisher-metric fact that chi-square is the local quadratic form of KL. It makes the routine substitution of G² for χ² an explicit statement with a computable error rather than folklore, and it is the kind of result that trains you to read the difference between two supposedly equivalent statistics as a curvature term. Short and self-contained.
- Cost: 25 min, easy-moderate, needs the delta method and basic information theory.
- Pairs with: finalists D1 and D2, all three treating a correction or difference term as a geometric object.

### 4. On the Information Required for Feedback Control
Matthew P. Leighton, Jose M. Betancourt, Thierry Emonet, Benjamin B. Machta, Michael C. Abbott. arXiv:2607.16639, 2026-07-18. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16639

- The move: derive a lower bound on the information rate from a controlled stochastic system to its controller that is required to hold a target steady state, giving performance-information Pareto frontiers, and saturated for state-independent passive dynamics by an explicit protocol that probabilistically time-reverses the passive dynamics.
- Why it is worth your time: information geometry meeting control and biology, from the group that has been pushing sloppiness and criticality. The bound is the load-bearing object, and the optimal protocol has a clean reading, run the passive drift backward, that you can hold in your head. It is the strongest cross-pollination signal in the section: control energy, biological sensing, and stochastic thermodynamics in one frontier.
- Cost: 75 min, hard, needs stochastic thermodynamics, information theory, and optimal control.
- Pairs with: the criticality and control-energy thread, and finalists D1 and D2 on the geometric side.

### 5. Information and the Accuracy Attainable in the Estimation of Statistical Parameters (older classic)
C. Radhakrishna Rao. Bulletin of the Calcutta Mathematical Society 37:81-91, 1945; reprinted in Breakthroughs in Statistics, Springer, 1992, pp. 235-247. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-0919-5_16

- The move: the paper that proved the Cramér-Rao bound and, in the same breath, introduced Fisher information as a Riemannian metric on the space of distributions, the origin of the Fisher-Rao geometry every paper above rests on.
- Why it is worth your time: everyone invokes Fisher-Rao and almost no one reads the eleven pages where Rao actually put the metric on the manifold. Reading it shows how naturally the geometry falls out of a question about estimation accuracy, which is exactly the register the correlation-prior, Killing-form, and GNS papers keep returning to. This is the ur-sleeper of the domain, and it beats several recent options on depth per page.
- Cost: 45 min, moderate, needs maximum likelihood and basic differential geometry; the prose is classical but clear.
- Pairs with: finalists D1, D2, and A2, every Fisher-Rao appearance this week traces back to here.

## Threads

### Thread 1: Name the estimand, or the number means nothing
The resolution profile (A4), the target-study-within-a-target-trial for disparities (C1), the nutritional-models paper (C3), and the hazards of hazard ratios (C5) are one argument applied to four reported quantities. A subgroup count, a disparity effect, a nutritional regression coefficient, and a hazard ratio all get published as if they were population targets, and each paper shows they are not well defined until you specify the question, or, in C5's case, that even a standard, universally reported summary can lack any causal meaning. A1 and A5 are the Bayesian flank of the same idea: a predictive limit or a posterior can concentrate on an object you did not intend. The tension worth holding is that A4, C1, and C3 say the fix is to define the estimand more carefully, while C5 says some familiar estimands are structurally broken no matter how carefully you define them, so definition is necessary but not sufficient.

### Thread 2: Fisher-Rao is the shared substrate, and its corrections are the content
Rao 1945 (D5) is literally the source, and A2, D1, D2, and D3 are four uses of the metric it introduced. A2 builds a prior from Fisher arc-length, D1 shows the quantum-classical gap is a Killing form sitting on top of the Fisher-Rao metric, D2 constructs the metric and its two-form from the GNS representation, and D3 recovers chi-square as the local Fisher form of mutual information. The split that makes this more than a list: A2 and D5 use the metric itself as the ground-truth object, while D1, D2, and D3 are all about the difference or correction term, the quantum piece, the antisymmetric two-form, the second-order remainder, treating what the base metric misses as the real information. Flatness and its ground truth on one side, curvature and correction on the other, the same tension that ran through last week's geometry cluster.

### Thread 3: The exact price, and who sits on the wrong side of it
B2, B3, B4, A3, and A5 are all sharp boundaries rather than one-sided guarantees. The bandit lower bound (B2) proves curvature costs a specific d^{1/4}, the Glauber estimator (B3) matches the information-theoretic signal rate without paying mixing time, and the inexact-score characterization (B4) proves sub-Gaussian error is not just sufficient but necessary. A3 and A5 are the same genre from the statistics side: the bootstrap is inconsistent on a precise list of problems, and nonparametric Bayes is inconsistent for natural priors used too hard. The disagreement across the two halves is the interesting part. The learning-theory converses find the boundary sitting exactly where it should, at the optimal rate, so the methods are as good as possible, whereas the Bayesian and bootstrap results find popular defaults sitting on the wrong side of their boundary, doing worse than practitioners assume. One camp certifies optimality, the other certifies failure, using the same converse-style reasoning.

## Synthesis

This week the useful content of a method is either the question it forces you to state precisely or the correction term the base object leaves out. The estimand cluster (A4, C1, C3, C5, with A1 and A5 behind it) argues that a great deal of applied inference reports numbers that are not well-defined targets, and that the discipline of writing down an ideal or target trial is really the discipline of refusing to estimate before the question exists. The geometry cluster (D5 underneath A2, D1, D2, D3) argues the complementary point in a different register: the interesting quantity is usually not the Fisher-Rao metric everyone shares but the correction to it, the symplectic Killing form or the antisymmetric two-form or the second-order remainder, which is where the quantum, the noncommutative, or the higher-order structure actually lives. The learning-theory section (B2, B3, B4, plus the classics B5, A3, A5) supplies the epistemics both need, sharp converses that say not just that something works but exactly where it stops, and it splits cleanly into results that certify optimality and results that certify the failure of a trusted default. Where they genuinely disagree is on temperament. The estimand and geometry papers trust structure, name the question or the invariant and the rest follows, while the converse papers trust boundaries, assume nothing is safe until you have proved where it breaks. Leighton and colleagues (D4) sit at the seam, deriving an information lower bound whose saturating control protocol is to time-reverse the passive dynamics, which is both a sharp converse and a piece of geometry, and it connects directly to the control-energy and criticality questions worth sitting with. Two of those questions. First, if the quantum correction to an information metric is a fixed Lie-algebraic invariant (D1), is the analogous correction in a learning or control problem also an invariant you could name in advance rather than bound after the fact. Second, if the information required to hold a nonequilibrium steady state has a clean lower bound (D4), does an aging or degrading system read as one steadily losing the budget to pay it, and would that give a control-energy diagnostic for when the steady state becomes unholdable.
