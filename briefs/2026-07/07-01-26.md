## A. Bayesian statistics

### 1. Inverse Probability Weighting in a Post-Bayesian World
Owen Thomas, William Denault, Valeria Vitelli. arXiv:2606.28685, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28685

- The move: IPW is recast as a reweighting of the KL divergence between model and truth inside a generalized belief posterior, so a frequentist bias correction becomes a coherent Bayesian object.
- Why it is worth your time: Bayesian handling of selection bias has always been awkward because reweighting samples feels un-Bayesian. This paper shows the weights live naturally in the loss that defines a Gibbs posterior, then proves convergence and posterior properties, and closes with a real PSA registry example where the correction matters. The taste it builds is seeing IPW as a statement about which divergence you are minimizing, not a trick applied after the fact.
- Cost: 45 min, moderate, needs comfort with generalized Bayes and Gibbs posteriors.
- Pairs with: the reformulation-invariance paper in section D, which argues KL is the only divergence that survives renaming, and the closed-form GVI paper below.

### 2. To select or not to select: predictively consistent priors instead of model selection
Anna Elisabeth Riha, Leevi Lindgren, David Kohns, Paul-Christian Bürkner, Aki Vehtari. arXiv:2606.22850, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22850

- The move: model selection can hurt predictive performance, and whether it helps is mostly a symptom of prior choice, so specify priors that keep prior predictive implications stable as complexity grows instead of selecting simpler models.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a negative result aimed at a reflex. The paper formalizes predictively consistent priors and shows across regression, forward selection, and nonlinear models that flexible models with such priors match or beat selected simpler ones. When selection does help, they read it as a diagnostic of bad joint prior implications rather than a win. It replaces the sparsity-at-the-component-level instinct with a demand on predictive space.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate.
- Pairs with: Bayesian workflow critiques and the general question of when parsimony is a modeling goal versus an artifact of the prior.

### 3. Closed-form solutions to some generalized variational inference problems
Hien Duy Nguyen, Jacob Westerhout. arXiv:2606.25492, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25492

- The move: solve the unrestricted, measure-level generalized variational inference problem for f-divergence, Bregman, and Rényi regularizers, giving explicit optimal densities instead of restricting to a parametric family first.
- Why it is worth your time: most GVI work jumps straight to a variational family, which hides what the regularizer actually does to the posterior. Here the Donsker-Varadhan characterization of the ordinary posterior is generalized cleanly, with a scalar inverse-gradient density formula for f-divergences and a truncated-power form for Rényi penalties. This is a paper you can teach from, and it tells you exactly how each divergence deforms the standard Bayesian answer.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs measure theory and convex duality.
- Pairs with: the post-Bayesian IPW paper, which is one specific choice of loss and divergence inside this same family.

### 4. Leveraging tails for adaptation
Sergios Agapiou, Ismaël Castillo, Paul Egels. arXiv:2606.20480, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20480

- The move: heavier prior tails give better nonparametric posterior contraction, and in a p-to-zero regime for p-exponential priors you get full adaptation to smoothness up to log factors, including for overparametrized shallow ReLU networks.
- Why it is worth your time: the folklore is that heavy tails are a robustness convenience. This shows they are the mechanism behind smoothness adaptation, with a sharp handle on how contraction improves as the tail index drops. The ReLU corollary, that an overparametrized shallow network adapts to any regularity between 0 and 2, is the kind of clean bridge from Bayesian nonparametrics to neural network theory that is worth internalizing.
- Cost: 75 min, hard, needs posterior contraction machinery.
- Pairs with: the singular learning paper in section B, another take on why overparametrized networks prefer simple functions.

### 5. Measurement Induced Confounding
George Perrett, Klint Kanopka. arXiv:2606.28774, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28774

- The move: adjusting for a latent confounder via sum scores, ability estimates, or item responses propagates measurement error into the treatment effect and yields biased estimates with miscalibrated coverage, and a Bayesian joint model of measurement, treatment, and response fixes it.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a negative result about standard practice in the social and medical sciences, where latent traits like motivation or ability are the confounders you most want to adjust for. The paper names the failure precisely and shows the conventional plug-in adjustments are the problem, not the solution. The fix, estimating all three models jointly so uncertainty flows correctly, is the honest Bayesian answer.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs latent variable and causal adjustment basics.
- Pairs with: the target trial section, since this is the measurement-error version of a confounding-control failure, and with psychometric measurement work.

## B. Learning theory and high-dimensional optimization

### 1. Singular Learning and Occam's Razor in Deep Monomial Networks
Kathlén Kohn, Giovanni Luca Marchetti, Farhan Shabir, Vahid Shahverdi, Weisheng Wang. arXiv:2606.28464, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28464

- The move: for deep networks with monomial activations, the pronounced singularities of singular learning theory occur exactly at subnetworks where neurons are inactive or redundant, proved with polynomial algebra and Mason's theorem.
- Why it is worth your time: singular learning theory usually stays abstract, so pinning criticality to a concrete architectural feature is real progress. Identifying the singular locus with subnetworks gives a clean algebraic account of implicit bias toward simpler functions, without hand-waving about flatness. The use of Mason's theorem is the kind of cross-import that does load-bearing work rather than decoration.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs some algebraic geometry and SLT vocabulary.
- Pairs with: the Bayesian tail-adaptation paper, and any work on degenerate minima and the learning coefficient.

### 2. SGD at the Edge of Stability: Stochastic Stabilization with Large Learning Rates
Konstantinos Emmanouilidis, Lachlan MacDonald, Salma Tarmoun, Rene Vidal. arXiv:2606.30930, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30930

- The move: for cross-entropy with linear classifiers and two-layer networks, SGD noise makes the dynamics alternate between a curvature-driven edge-of-stability regime and a stable regime, and SGD provably self-stabilizes and returns to stability in a fixed number of steps.
- Why it is worth your time: most edge-of-stability analysis is deterministic, which quietly assumes away the mechanism that matters in practice. This gives sharp best-iterate convergence with large step sizes and explains the oscillate-then-settle behavior you actually see. It is a mechanism for why large learning rates work, not a bound that tolerates them.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs optimization dynamics background.
- Pairs with: the spurious-feature XOR paper below, which also reads training as a sequence of phases with couplings between them.

### 3. SGD Provably Prioritizes a Shortcut Spurious Feature in the XOR Model
Tyler LaBonte, Vidya Muthukumar. arXiv:2606.30444, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30444

- The move: an end-to-end characterization for two-layer ReLU networks on XOR-plus-linear-shortcut data showing SGD learns the spurious feature first and exponentially fast, with the spurious and signal dynamics coupled so a stronger shortcut suppresses signal learning.
- Why it is worth your time: shortcut learning is usually studied empirically, and mitigation methods rest on unproven assumptions about the learned representation. This derives the phenomenon from the dynamics, identifies explicit phase transitions, and shows the shortcut can dominate even at the sample complexity threshold where XOR would otherwise be learnable. That is exactly the kind of result that should change how you design de-biasing methods.
- Cost: 55 min, hard, needs high-dimensional SGD analysis.
- Pairs with: the edge-of-stability paper, and the measurement-induced confounding paper in section A as a spurious-association echo in a different field.

### 4. Blackwell Approachability and Gradient Equilibrium are Equivalent
Brian W. Lee, Nika Haghtalab, Michael I. Jordan, Ryan J. Tibshirani. arXiv:2606.27315, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27315

- The move: gradient equilibrium, the recent framework abstracting online conformal prediction, is algorithmically equivalent to Blackwell approachability, with efficient two-way reductions that preserve error rates.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a reframe that snaps a new object onto an old one and thereby onto the whole web of equivalences between approachability, regret minimization, and calibration. The reductions are not just existence proofs, they transfer refined guarantees like optimism and strong adaptivity from regret minimization into gradient equilibrium. Reframes like this compound, which is why it belongs here over any single new bound.
- Cost: 50 min, moderate to hard, needs online learning background.
- Pairs with: online conformal prediction and calibration, and the broader question of which online objectives are secretly the same.

### 5. Fast algorithms for learning a Gaussian under halfspace truncation with optimal sample complexity
Haitong Liu, Deepak Narayanan Sridharan, David Steurer, Manuel Wiedmer. arXiv:2606.27298, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27298

- The move: learning a high-dimensional Gaussian truncated to an unknown halfspace can be done with the optimal number of samples and in time dominated by computing the empirical covariance, so under nontrivial truncation you learn the Gaussian essentially for free.
- Why it is worth your time: the surprise is that truncation costs you nothing in the leading order, once you reinterpret the low-degree moments through a relative truncation parameter that uniquely fixes the untruncated Gaussian. This sidesteps the projected SGD procedure that prior work leaned on. It is a sharp sample-and-time-complexity result with a clean idea underneath, the good end of learning theory that touches real estimation.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs concentration and moment methods.
- Pairs with: truncated statistics and robust estimation, and identifiability results generally.

## C. Target trial emulation

### 1. The Ideal Trial: Defining Causal Estimands that Balance Relevance and Feasibility in Target Trial Emulations and Actual Randomized Trials
Margarita Moreno-Betancur, Rushani Wijesuriya, John B. Carlin. Epidemiology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001933) | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41490058/

- The move: separate the ideal trial, the hypothetical perfect experiment that defines the estimand of real interest, from the target trial that is aligned to the available data, and show the popular aligned approach can retain biases mirroring the often-ignored biases of actual randomized trials.
- Why it is worth your time: this sharpens what a target trial actually is, which the spec names as the central skill in this domain. The argument that aligning the target trial to your data trades relevance for feasibility, and hides bias in the gap, is the kind of distinction that changes how you specify a study. It puts target trial emulation and real trials on the same footing under one ideal.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs the estimand and target trial framing.
- Pairs with: the anatomy-of-an-estimand commentary below, and the ideal versus aligned tension runs through the whole section.

### 2. Clarifying Causal Questions in Population Health Research: Anatomy of a Causal Estimand
Koichiro Shiba. American Journal of Epidemiology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwag079) | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41974452/

- The move: dissect a causal estimand into four components, treatment definition, comparator, intervention strategy, and summary measure, and argue for the natural course as comparator and for sustained or stochastic interventions over deterministic elimination.
- Why it is worth your time: this sits in the exact register of the Hernán reference paper the spec anchors on, and it is specific rather than a checklist. The point that complex social exposures break the consistency assumption through multiple versions of treatment, and that a natural-course comparator reflects population impact better than a fixed counterfactual level, is a genuine sharpening of the causal question. It also pushes estimands past population averages toward disparity effects.
- Cost: 30 min, easy to moderate.
- Pairs with: the ideal trial paper, and estimand specification work in the Bayesian causal literature.

### 3. Target trial emulation without matching: a more efficient approach for evaluating vaccine effectiveness using observational data
Emily Wu, Elizabeth Rogawski McQuade, Mats Stensrud, Razieh Nabi, David Benkeser. Epidemiology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001982) | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41911278/

- The move: matching-based vaccine effectiveness emulations obscure the estimand and lose efficiency, so define a calendar-time-summarized effectiveness estimand and estimate it with two hazard regressions instead of matching.
- Why it is worth your time: the paper is honest that matching is popular because it is simple, then shows what that simplicity costs when uptake and infection dynamics both move over calendar time. Defining the estimand to summarize effectiveness over calendar time, the way efficacy is summarized in a randomized trial, is the clarifying step, and the efficiency gains are real in their COVID example. Good example of the framing changing both the target and the estimator.
- Cost: 45 min, moderate to hard, needs survival and estimand background.
- Pairs with: immortal time and time-zero issues, and the person-time paper below.

### 4. Evaluation of potential approaches for counting person-time in instances where no active comparator is present
Katherine Giorgio, Pamela L. Lutsey, Wendy Wang, Rob Walker, Faye L. Norby, Jared Huling. American Journal of Epidemiology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwaf266) | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41332280/

- The move: when there is no active comparator, six ways to assign surrogate index dates to untreated patients are tested against a known reference, and only rejection sampling recovers a nearly unbiased effect while median, prediction-model, and matching approaches fail badly.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a concrete negative result about time zero, the place immortal time bias hides. Using a converted sulfonylurea arm as a synthetic untreated group to get a reference hazard ratio is a clean experimental design, and the spread of estimates from 0.61 to 1.15 against a reference of 0.69 is a blunt warning. It teaches you which fixes for the no-active-comparator problem are safe.
- Cost: 35 min, moderate.
- Pairs with: immortal time bias and the general problem of assigning time zero without a treatment event.

### 5. Target trial emulation under nonmutually exclusive assignment: structural pitfalls and methodological remedies
Atsushi Takayama, Shiro Tanaka, Koji Kawakami. American Journal of Epidemiology, 2026. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwag014) | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41555711/

- The move: when treatment groups are not mutually exclusive, patients on combination or single-agent or no therapy, a simulation study shows substantial bias unless overlap and positivity are handled explicitly, but with sufficient covariate overlap the nonexclusive design can match or beat the exclusive one.
- Why it is worth your time: the mutually exclusive exposure assumption is so standard it is rarely stated, and this paper surfaces what breaks when it fails. The nuance is the payoff: nonexclusive assignment is not automatically worse, it depends on overlap, so the lesson is to align estimand, design, and assignment structure rather than to avoid the design. That is a subtler bias than the usual confounding story.
- Cost: 35 min, moderate.
- Pairs with: positivity and overlap diagnostics, and propensity score estimation under ambiguous exposure.

## D. Information geometry

### 1. Group invariance of f-divergences and the Fisher-Rao distance
Frank Nielsen, Kazuki Okamura. arXiv:2606.25790, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25790

- The move: in a transformation model where a group acts on both sample and parameter spaces, every f-divergence is invariant under the action and therefore depends only on a maximal invariant of the parameter pair, a double coset when the action is transitive, and the same reduction applies to the Fisher-Rao distance.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a clean structural result you can teach from, and it does real work. It reduces divergence computation between two distributions to a much smaller invariant, which for location-scale families collapses a hard distance to a function of a single quantity. The lesson is to look for the group before you compute the geometry.
- Cost: 40 min, moderate, needs group actions and exponential families.
- Pairs with: the reformulation-invariance paper below, another symmetry-first account of which divergence to use.

### 2. Reformulation Invariance and the Axiomatic Foundations of Inference
Raphaël Trésor, Thijs van de Laar, Bert de Vries. arXiv:2606.21551, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21551

- The move: require only that an inference method give the same answer when the same problem is renamed or restated, and this single invariance forces inference to be divergence minimization and successively narrows f-divergences to alpha-divergences to KL alone.
- Why it is worth your time: this derives the uniqueness of KL from a weaker and more transparent premise than the Shore-Johnson or Csiszár axioms, and it recasts inference as selecting a least element under a preorder rather than minimizing a number. The categorical framing, with reformulations as morphisms and the inference operator as a functor mirroring Cencov's characterization of the Fisher metric, is the geometry doing the proving. A strong reframe of what a divergence is.
- Cost: 60 min, hard, needs comfort with abstraction and some category theory.
- Pairs with: the f-divergence invariance paper above, and the post-Bayesian IPW and closed-form GVI papers in section A, which both hinge on divergence choice.

### 3. Ricci flow for the Bures-Helstrom qubit metric
Andrew Lesniewski. arXiv:2606.19493, June 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19493

- The move: compute the Ricci flow of the Bures-Helstrom metric on the qubit state space explicitly, showing that because the metric is Einstein the flow is a homothetic shrinker that stays inside the monotone cone until a finite extinction time, with the linearized flow governed by a shifted three-sphere Laplacian.
- Why it is worth your time: this is geometry used to prove something specific about a quantum statistical manifold, not manifold vocabulary bolted onto a method. Identifying the Bloch ball with a geodesic hemisphere of the round three-sphere and reading off the spectral gap is exactly the kind of concrete calculation that builds intuition for quantum Fisher information. Physics anchors the object, the geometry carries the result.
- Cost: 70 min, hard, needs Riemannian geometry and quantum information basics.
- Pairs with: thermodynamic and fidelity geometry, and dynamical-systems views of statistical manifolds.

### 4. Information Geometry via the Q-Root Transform
Levin Maier. arXiv:2603.20081, March 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20081

- The move: build an infinite-dimensional l-p information geometry in which a q-root transform is an isometry, construct the l-2 and l-q Fisher-Rao geometries with Amari-Cencov connections, and solve a linear optimization gradient flow explicitly as the geodesic flow of a geodesically complete exponential connection tied to an integrable Hamiltonian system.
- Why it is worth your time: the square-root transform that trivializes the Fisher-Rao geometry of densities gets generalized, and the payoff is an explicit, provably convergent gradient flow with a completely integrable structure and a momentum map. The geometry is not decoration, it is what makes the flow solvable. This is the durable, timeless-leaning pick of the section, useful well past this news cycle.
- Cost: 80 min, hard, needs infinite-dimensional geometry and Hamiltonian mechanics.
- Pairs with: natural gradient methods and the geometry of density spaces.

### 5. An equivalence of moment closure and nonlinear variational approximation of the Fokker-Planck equation for dilute polymeric flow
Caroline Lasser, Stephan B. Lunowa, Barbara Wohlmuth. arXiv:2602.04644, February 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04644

- The move: in the linearized Hookean setting, classical moment closure for the Fokker-Planck equation of polymeric flow is exactly the Dirac-Frankel variational approximation on a Gaussian manifold with the Fisher-Rao metric, and the manifold's invariance under the dynamics recovers the Oldroyd-B closure exactly.
- Why it is worth your time: this is a reframe that identifies an ad hoc engineering closure with a geometric projection, which is the test the spec sets: remove the Fisher-Rao structure and the equivalence collapses. Even limited to the linear case, it gives an abstract error representation and a principled route to reduced schemes for nonlinear forcing. Seeing moment closure as variational projection onto a statistical manifold is the transferable move.
- Cost: 55 min, hard, needs kinetic theory and variational approximation.
- Pairs with: the q-root and f-divergence papers above, and reduced-order modeling of kinetic equations.

## Threads

### Divergence geometry is one object seen from four sides
Post-Bayesian IPW (A1), closed-form GVI (A3), f-divergence invariance (D1), and reformulation invariance (D2) are all arguing about which divergence you should minimize and why. A1 uses a reweighted KL to absorb selection bias, A3 works out what each f-, Bregman-, and Rényi-divergence does to the posterior in closed form, D1 shows symmetry collapses any f-divergence to a maximal invariant, and D2 argues that renaming invariance alone forces KL. The tension worth sitting with: A3 studies the whole divergence family as a design space while D2 says only KL is coherent, so the practical freedom in generalized Bayes may be exactly the coherence D2 rules out.

### Overparametrized models prefer simple functions, and now we have mechanisms
Tail adaptation (A4), singular learning in monomial networks (B1), and the spurious-feature XOR dynamics (B3) each give a mechanism for implicit simplicity rather than a bound. A4 makes heavy tails the driver of smoothness adaptation, B1 locates the singular geometry at subnetworks and reads Occam's razor off the algebra, and B3 shows the dynamics reach the simple shortcut first. Together they suggest the bias toward simplicity is real but not always benign, since the same pull that adapts to smoothness in A4 is the pull that grabs a spurious feature in B3.

### Time zero and the estimand are the whole game in causal inference
The ideal trial (C1), anatomy of an estimand (C2), matching-free vaccine effectiveness (C3), person-time without an active comparator (C4), and nonexclusive assignment (C5) are all about pinning down the causal question before the data touch it. C1 and C2 sharpen what estimand you are after, C3 shows the estimand choice changes the estimator, and C4 and C5 show that sloppiness about time zero or exposure structure produces bias that no downstream adjustment repairs. Measurement-induced confounding (A5) is the same failure in psychometric clothing, where the mismeasured confounder, not the design, is what breaks identification.

### Geometry as a proof engine versus geometry as decoration
The spec's sharpest test for section D is whether removing the geometry collapses the result, and this week four papers pass it. Ricci flow on the qubit metric (D3), the q-root transform (D4), and the moment-closure equivalence (D5) each need the manifold structure to get their result, and B1's singular geometry is load-bearing in the same way. Set against A1 and D1, the throughline is that geometry earns its place when it turns a computation you were doing anyway into a statement about invariants or flows.

## Synthesis

The strongest current this week is that divergence choice is not a modeling detail, it is the object. Four papers across Bayesian statistics and information geometry converge on this from opposite directions, and they disagree in a useful way: the closed-form GVI work treats the divergence family as a rich design space while reformulation invariance argues that coherence leaves you only KL. That disagreement is worth holding open, because generalized Bayes lives or dies on whether the extra freedom is principled or a symptom of dropping an axiom. The learning theory picks are unusually mechanistic, replacing bounds with dynamics: edge of stability self-stabilizes, SGD grabs shortcuts first, singular geometry sits at subnetworks, and truncation turns out to be free. The recurring lesson is that the implicit bias toward simple solutions is a double-edged mechanism, the same force that gives smoothness adaptation also gives shortcut learning. Target trial emulation had a genuinely methodological week, and its message rhymes with the divergence thread: fix the question, the ideal trial, the estimand, the time zero, before you touch the estimator, because bias introduced at specification is not recoverable later. Measurement-induced confounding makes the same point in a different field, that a mismeasured confounder corrupts the effect no matter how you adjust. If there is one question to carry forward, it is whether the geometric and causal notions of invariance are the same idea, since Cencov's characterization of the Fisher metric and the target trial's insistence on a well-posed estimand both amount to demanding that the answer not depend on how you happened to write the problem down. The second, for the learning theory side, is whether the simplicity bias can be steered, so that the mechanism that adapts to smoothness can be made to ignore the shortcut.
