## A. Bayesian statistics

### 1. Nonparametric Bayesian Inference for Partially Identified Discrete Response Models
Elie Tamer, Christopher D. Walker. arXiv:2608.25814, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25814

- **The move:** Put a Gaussian process prior on the observable conditional pmf, push it through the identification map, and Bayesian inference for the identified set falls out, with posterior consistency for the true set.
- **Why it is worth your time:** In partially identified models the likelihood is flat across the identified set, so a parametric prior secretly does the identifying and you cannot tell data from assumption. Tamer and Walker keep the prior on the thing the data actually constrain, the conditional distribution, and get a posterior on the set itself with closed-form sampling. It is the honest way to be Bayesian about partial identification: the prior shapes the observable, not the answer.
- **Cost:** ~50 min, moderate to hard, needs comfort with partial identification and GP priors.
- **Pairs with:** Greenland and Robins on exchangeability and identifiability in the target trial section; Manski bounds.

### 2. Exploiting Exact Conditionals Improves Conditioning: Provably Fast Mixing Time Bounds By Sampling from the Marginal
Abhijit Chowdhary, Federica Milinanni, Julianne Chung, Elizabeth Newman. arXiv:2608.27884, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27884

- **The move:** When the target factors as marginal times an exact conditional, Metropolis-Hastings on the marginal followed by an exact conditional draw provably mixes faster than joint or one-block sampling.
- **Why it is worth your time:** A clean mechanism, not a nicer trace plot. Sampling the low-dimensional marginal and completing with the tractable conditional strips the badly conditioned directions out of the chain, and MarCo proves the mixing-time gain through Peskun-Tierney ordering. The lesson is that "condition on what you can integrate out" is a statement about conditioning number and mixing, not just a modeling convenience.
- **Cost:** ~40 min, moderate, needs Markov chain comparison arguments.
- **Pairs with:** the natural-gradient message passing paper in the geometry section, which is the same marginal-versus-conditional bookkeeping on a factor graph.

### 3. Stochastic Bayes factors: why, when, and how
Leonardo Egidi, Ioannis Ntzoufras. arXiv:2608.29154, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29154

- **The move:** Treat the Bayes factor as a random variable by replicating data from the posterior predictive, so model comparison returns a distribution instead of a single fragile number.
- **Why it is worth your time:** A Bayes factor hides how sensitive it is to the one sample you happened to see. Propagating replication uncertainty exposes when a "decisive" factor is an artifact of a single dataset and when it is stable, which turns model comparison into a calibration question about the factor's own sampling behavior. The paper is honest about when the construction helps and when it does not.
- **Cost:** ~30 min, easy to moderate.
- **Pairs with:** Dawid on calibration below; poscosea sensitivity.

### 4. poscosea: A Computationally Efficient Sensitivity Analysis for Bayesian Models using the posterior covariance representation
Yusaku Ohkubo, Yukito Iba. arXiv:2608.25426, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25426

- **The move:** Approximate leave-k-out and bootstrap diagnostics from a single posterior covariance, no refitting.
- **Why it is worth your time:** This is the infinitesimal jackknife done inside the posterior. Perturbations of the data or model show up as posterior covariances between log-likelihood contributions and the quantity of interest, so you buy leave-k-out and bootstrap-style sensitivity for the price of one fit. Iba's taste shows in framing it as a property of the posterior geometry under misspecification rather than a computational trick.
- **Cost:** ~35 min, moderate.
- **Pairs with:** Bayesian workflow and model criticism; the denoising score matching loss floor, which also reads off a second-order object.

### 5. The Well-Calibrated Bayesian (classic, under-read)
A. P. Dawid. Journal of the American Statistical Association 77(379):605-613, 1982. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1982.10477856

- **The move:** A coherent Bayesian expects to be well calibrated, and taking that expectation seriously is destructive for the theory of coherence.
- **Why it is worth your time:** Dawid proves the reassuring half, that coherence implies you expect calibration, then the destructive half, that a Bayesian who actually checks calibration and updates on it cannot remain coherent. This is the origin of the tension between being internally consistent and being externally right that every posterior predictive check and every conformal method is still negotiating. Read it to see the problem stated cleanly before the modern machinery buried it.
- **Cost:** ~40 min, moderate, foundational.
- **Pairs with:** the stochastic Bayes factor above; anytime-valid and conformal calibration.

## B. Learning theory and high-dimensional optimization

### 1. The Multiple Timescales of Gradient Descent on the Edge of Stability: A Perturbative Derivation of the Central Flow
Raphaël Berthier. arXiv:2609.01034, Sep 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01034

- **The move:** Derive the central flow of edge-of-stability gradient descent as a singular-perturbation limit of a loss written as f = g + epsilon h, with three separated timescales.
- **Why it is worth your time:** The central flow was an empirical description of how gradient descent behaves once the sharpness pins at 2 over the step size. Berthier gives it a derivation: split the loss into a fast oscillating part and a slow drift, and the constrained dynamics emerge as epsilon goes to zero. The payoff is turning a phenomenological picture into something provable, and seeing that the mechanism is timescale separation, not anything special to neural networks.
- **Cost:** ~1 hr, hard, needs singular perturbation and averaging.
- **Pairs with:** Yoshizawa on gradient flows in the geometry section; prior weeks' quadratic-model edge-of-stability work.

### 2. Asymptotics for Model Selection in Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis
Mathias Drton, Andrew McCormack, Daniel Windisch. arXiv:2608.23513, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23513

- **The move:** At the PPCA singularities where the Fisher information drops rank, the marginal likelihood is not the usual BIC, and the singular BIC removes the overpenalization.
- **Why it is worth your time:** Standard BIC assumes a regular model, but PPCA at the true rank is singular, so the effective dimension is not the parameter count and BIC overpenalizes and underselects components. Using Watanabe's singular learning theory the authors compute the real asymptotic and get sBIC, which recovers the right rank. A concrete case where singular learning theory pays rent on a model people actually run.
- **Cost:** ~50 min, hard, needs BIC asymptotics and a little algebraic geometry.
- **Pairs with:** Amari 1982 and Jeffreys in the geometry section; Yoshizawa's singular information geometry, which resolves exactly these degeneracies.

### 3. How Many Samples Are Needed to Determine Causal Direction? Sharp Minimax Bounds for Bivariate LiNGAM
Jikai Jin. arXiv:2608.15840, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15840

- **The move:** Sharp local minimax sample complexity for orienting X to Y versus Y to X, as a joint function of effect strength, distance from Gaussianity, and scale uncertainty.
- **Why it is worth your time:** LiNGAM identifiability is usually stated as a binary, non-Gaussianity identifies direction. This makes it quantitative: how many samples the orientation costs, and how the cost blows up as the noise approaches Gaussian or the scales become uncertain. It cleanly separates identification that comes from non-Gaussian dependence from what leaks in through covariance structure. A rare sharp sample-complexity result on a causal-discovery primitive.
- **Cost:** ~45 min, moderate to hard.
- **Pairs with:** the target trial identification papers; ICA and LiNGAM.

### 4. Algorithmic Principles For Multiclass Learning Are Hard To Come By: Limits of Regularization and Proper Learning
Julian Asilis, Shaddin Dughmi, Vatsal Sharan, Alec Sun, Shang-Hua Teng, Chang Wang. arXiv:2608.26516, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26516

- **The move:** Two negative results: learnability does not reduce to proper learning by enlarging the hypothesis class, and regularization via structural risk minimization is not a universal learner.
- **Why it is worth your time:** In binary classification the comforting story is that ERM or SRM over the right class is essentially all you need. The multiclass world does not obey it: there are learnable problems no proper learner and no regularizer captures, and the paper characterizes when SRM still works. It kills a piece of folklore, which is exactly the kind of result that changes the reflex to "just regularize."
- **Cost:** ~50 min, hard, needs PAC and multiclass learning theory.
- **Pairs with:** Dawid's coherence-versus-calibration gap; prior weeks' information-based complexity.

### 5. Statistical mechanics of learning from examples (classic, under-read)
H. S. Seung, H. Sompolinsky, N. Tishby. Physical Review A 45:6056-6091, 1992. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.45.6056

- **The move:** Cast learning as a Gibbs distribution over networks at temperature T and compute generalization, including for rules the architecture cannot realize, via high-temperature and annealed approximations.
- **Why it is worth your time:** This is the source of the phase-transition view of learning that the criticality and double-descent literatures keep rediscovering. It handles the unrealizable case, target outside the hypothesis class, which is exactly where modern overparameterized talk gets sloppy, and it makes the transitions explicit with statistical mechanics. Read the original before the ideas were renamed.
- **Cost:** ~1.5 hr, hard, physics notation.
- **Pairs with:** Landau-theory-of-criticality treatments of in-context learning; edge-of-stability dynamics.

## C. Target trial emulation

New pure methodology was thin this week: the strongest recent estimand and immortal-time papers are already in this log. So this section is two fresh emulations chosen for a design move rather than a clinical finding, plus the three papers the whole field rests on. Records retrieved via the PubMed connector; DOIs below.

### 1. Lithium for the prevention of suicide in US veterans: a target trial emulation
BMJ Mental Health, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2026-302694

- **The move:** Emulate two target trials, initiation and continuation, and benchmark the initiation trial against the CSP-590 randomized trial before trusting the continuation estimand no feasible trial delivered.
- **Why it is worth your time:** The lesson is the benchmarking, not the drug. CSP-590 gives ground truth for the initiation effect, so the emulation reproduces it first and only then extends to the continuation, per-protocol, question. That is how you earn trust in an estimand with no RCT: calibrate on the one that has it. Suicide outcomes in VA data, so the stakes are real and the design discipline matters.
- **Cost:** ~30 min, easy to moderate.
- **Pairs with:** Robins and Finkelstein below, whose IPCW is what the continuation estimand needs.

### 2. Programmatic effectiveness of tuberculosis preventive treatment for people with HIV in six high-tuberculosis-burden countries (PROTECT)
Lancet HIV, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-3018(26)00140-2

- **The move:** Emulate the same target trial six times across six countries and meta-analyze, treating the emulation as a repeatable instrument rather than a one-off.
- **Why it is worth your time:** A single-database emulation cannot separate design bias from local truth. Running the identical protocol across six high-burden settings and combining gives the emulation a replication structure and shows where the effect travels and where it does not. It is a transportability argument dressed as a programmatic evaluation.
- **Cost:** ~30 min, easy to moderate.
- **Pairs with:** prior weeks' work on extending trial inferences to target populations.

### 3. Specifying a target trial prevents immortal time bias and other self-inflicted injuries in observational analyses (classic)
Miguel A. Hernán, Brian C. Sauer, Sonia Hernández-Díaz, Robert Platt, Ian Shrier. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 79:70-75, 2016. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2016.04.014

- **The move:** Most observational biases are self-inflicted misalignments of eligibility, treatment assignment, and start of follow-up, and writing the target trial protocol prevents them by construction.
- **Why it is worth your time:** The cleanest short statement of why the framework exists. Immortal time, prevalent user, and depletion biases are not separate monsters, they are all failures to set time zero coherently. If you read one thing to internalize the design-first stance, this is it.
- **Cost:** ~20 min, easy, foundational.
- **Pairs with:** everything else in the domain.

### 4. Identifiability, exchangeability, and epidemiological confounding (classic, under-read)
Sander Greenland, James M. Robins. International Journal of Epidemiology 15(3):413-419, 1986. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/15.3.413

- **The move:** Confounding is a failure of identifiability, and confounder control smuggles in an exchangeability assumption; the paper draws the logical link in a single deterministic model.
- **Why it is worth your time:** It ties the epidemiologist's confounding, the Bayesian's exchangeability, and the statistician's identifiability together. That is the bridge to the Bayesian section: the same exchangeability de Finetti uses to justify a prior is what a target trial assumes when it claims comparability of exposure groups. Load-bearing and still under-read by the machine-learning causal crowd.
- **Cost:** ~30 min, moderate, foundational.
- **Pairs with:** Tamer's partial identification and Dawid's coherence; de Finetti.

### 5. Correcting for noncompliance and dependent censoring in an AIDS Clinical Trial with inverse probability of censoring weighted (IPCW) log-rank tests (classic)
James M. Robins, Dianne M. Finkelstein. Biometrics 56(3):779-788, 2000. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0006-341x.2000.00779.x

- **The move:** Artificially censor patients when they stop or switch therapy, then reweight by inverse probability of censoring to recover the per-protocol effect that intent-to-treat hid.
- **Why it is worth your time:** This is the machinery under clone-censor-weight. In ACTG 021 the intent-to-treat comparison found nothing because of crossover, and IPCW recovered a real survival benefit by treating noncompliance as dependent censoring and weighting it away. Read it to see the estimator that every modern sustained-treatment emulation is quietly using.
- **Cost:** ~40 min, moderate to hard.
- **Pairs with:** the lithium continuation trial above; marginal structural models.

## D. Information geometry

### 1. Finite relaxation protocols with minimal dissipation
Ben Ansbacher, Harrison Hartle, Abhishek Yadav, Jan Korbel, David H. Wolpert. arXiv:2608.25207, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25207

- **The move:** The optimal finite sequence of quench-relax steps for work extraction converges to the Fisher-Rao geodesic, with the schedule set by a Lambert-W recurrence.
- **Why it is worth your time:** Thermodynamic length, the geodesic length in the Fisher-Rao metric, is usually the continuous-protocol ideal. This shows the finite-step optimum is not a hand-waved approximation, it converges to the geodesic on a schedule you can write down, and it gives a dissipation lower bound in terms of distance from equilibrium. The geometry does real work: remove the Fisher-Rao structure and there is no geodesic to converge to.
- **Cost:** ~45 min, moderate to hard, stochastic thermodynamics.
- **Pairs with:** thermodynamic length from prior weeks; optimal control and criticality.

### 2. The Loss Floor of Denoising Score Matching: Fisher Geometry from Schrödinger Bridges
Avinash Raju, Kai Zhang. arXiv:2608.23916, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23916

- **The move:** The irreducible denoising-score-matching training loss equals the trace of the Fisher-Rao metric of the conditional endpoint family, integrated along the diffusion trajectory.
- **Why it is worth your time:** People treat the DSM loss floor as noise. This identifies it as a geometric quantity, an arc-length-like integral of Fisher information along the bridge, which means the number you cannot train away is reporting the curvature of the conditional family, with a closed form in the Gaussian case. A genuine reframe connecting diffusion-model training to information geometry, and the test passes: kill the Fisher structure and the loss floor has no expression.
- **Cost:** ~45 min, hard, needs score matching and Schrödinger bridges.
- **Pairs with:** poscosea and Drton's sBIC, which also read a second-order Fisher object.

### 3. Information Geometry of Message Passing
Mykola Lukashchuk, Kyrylo Yemets, Alex Ledbetter, İsmail Şenöz. arXiv:2608.15922, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15922

- **The move:** The natural-gradient stationarity condition of variational inference is edge-local on a Forney factor graph, so belief propagation is natural-gradient projection, message by message.
- **Why it is worth your time:** Each belief-propagation message turns out to be the part of the exact belief that the receiving marginal family can represent, that is, a natural-gradient projection, and the proposed NGMP keeps more of the message when the family cannot represent it. This is the reframe that makes "message passing" and "natural gradient" the same object, and it predicts where standard variational message passing loses information, along long partially observed chains.
- **Cost:** ~45 min, moderate to hard, needs exponential families and factor graphs.
- **Pairs with:** MarCo's marginal-then-conditional sampling in the Bayesian section; natural gradient.

### 4. Information Geometry of Gradient Flows
Shintaro Yoshizawa. arXiv:2608.21152, Aug 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21152

- **The move:** Build dually flat geometry from log-determinant potentials on positive definite matrices and push it into a singular regime, using blow-up geometry to handle degenerate, non-Morse critical sets.
- **Why it is worth your time:** Most information geometry assumes a well-behaved dually flat manifold. This one goes where the structure degenerates, the same singularities that break BIC in the PPCA paper, and uses blow-ups to keep the geometry meaningful while connecting Fisher-Rao, Bregman, and Bures-Wasserstein flows. It is the geometric companion to singular learning theory: when the Fisher metric drops rank, resolve the singularity instead of giving up.
- **Cost:** ~1 hr, hard, differential geometry.
- **Pairs with:** Drton's sBIC and Berthier's central flow; Seung-Sompolinsky-Tishby phase transitions.

### 5. An invariant form for the prior probability in estimation problems (classic)
Harold Jeffreys. Proceedings of the Royal Society A 186(1007):453-461, 1946. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1946.0056

- **The move:** The invariant prior is the Riemannian volume element of the Fisher metric, so the uninformative prior is a statement about the geometry of the model, not about the parameters.
- **Why it is worth your time:** This is where information geometry quietly enters Bayesian statistics. Jeffreys picks the prior that is invariant to reparameterization, and that object is exactly the Fisher-Rao volume, which makes the geometry-to-Bayes bridge concrete and explains why any prior that ignores the metric is secretly choosing a parameterization. The 1946 argument is short and still the cleanest version.
- **Cost:** ~25 min, moderate, foundational.
- **Pairs with:** Greenland and Robins on exchangeability and Tamer on partial identification; the Fisher-trace objects in the thermodynamics and DSM papers.

## Threads

**1. Singularities and slow flows.** Drton sBIC, Yoshizawa gradient flows, Berthier central flow, Seung-Sompolinsky-Tishby. When the Fisher information drops rank, or the loss splits into a fast and a slow part, the naive object is wrong: BIC overpenalizes, the gradient is not the story, the generalization curve has a transition. The fix is to resolve the degeneracy or separate the timescales. Tension worth noting: Drton and Yoshizawa treat singularity as real structure to compute with, while Berthier's split is a benign perturbation that only looks singular. Two different senses of the word.

**2. One Fisher tensor, five jobs.** DSM loss floor, poscosea posterior covariance, Drton effective dimension, Jeffreys volume, Wolpert thermodynamic length. The same Fisher information appears as an irreducible loss, a sensitivity, an effective dimension, a prior, and a length. Worth sitting with which of these are genuinely the same object and which just share notation.

**3. Who is doing the identifying, the data or the assumption.** Tamer partial identification, Greenland-Robins exchangeability, Jin LiNGAM sample complexity, Jeffreys. Each asks what the data actually pin down versus what an assumption supplies, whether that assumption is a prior, an exchangeability claim, a non-Gaussianity condition, or a parameterization. Tamer keeps the two separate on purpose, Greenland and Robins expose the hidden exchangeability inside confounder control, Jin prices exactly how much non-Gaussianity buys an arrow.

**4. Coherence is not calibration, and benchmarking is the referee.** Dawid, stochastic Bayes factors, Asilis multiclass, the lithium and PROTECT emulations. Internal consistency does not imply external correctness: Dawid proves the gap, the stochastic Bayes factor measures a factor's fragility, the multiclass result kills the "just regularize" reflex, and the two emulations only earn their estimands by reproducing or replicating something external. The common move is refusing to take an internally consistent number at face value.

## Synthesis

The connective tissue this week is the Fisher information metric and what happens when it degenerates. Three papers, Drton, Yoshizawa, and by extension the denoising-score-matching floor, are really about the same event: the Fisher matrix losing rank, which breaks model selection, ordinary geometry, and the assumption that training loss can reach zero. The learning-theory picks argue that the useful description of a hard optimization is often a slower flow or a phase transition rather than a bound, which is the same instinct as thermodynamic length in the geometry section. On the causal side the theme is honesty about identification: Tamer refuses to let the prior pretend to identify a set, Greenland and Robins show that confounder control was an exchangeability assumption all along, and Jin prices how much non-Gaussianity buys a causal direction. The sharpest disagreement is about singularity itself: Drton and Yoshizawa treat it as real structure to resolve and compute with, while Berthier's edge-of-stability split is a benign perturbation that only looks singular. Target trial emulation is thin on new methodology, so it leans on the three papers the field rests on, and the one live idea there is benchmarking, that an emulation earns a novel estimand by first reproducing a trial we already trust. Dawid's 1982 result hangs over all of it: coherence does not imply calibration, and both the Bayes-factor and the target-trial papers are variations on not trusting an internally consistent number until something external checks it. Two questions worth sitting with. Is the Fisher tensor that shows up as loss floor, sensitivity, effective dimension, prior, and thermodynamic length one object doing five jobs, or five objects sharing a symbol. And when a model is singular, is resolving the singularity, through blow-ups or sBIC, always better than avoiding it. For the aging-as-a-dynamical-system work, the pairing to watch is singular learning theory with edge-of-stability flows: both say the interesting behavior lives exactly where the usual quadratic picture fails.
